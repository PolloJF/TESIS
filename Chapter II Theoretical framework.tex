\subsection{Quasi-Static Magnetic Field Regime}\label{subsec:quasi-static}
% (From: Cuasistatic Magnetic Field)
% TODO: Conditions for quasi-static approximation (r << lambda/2π), near-field decay (1/r^3) components.
In a quasistatic magnetic field regime, the electromagnetic fields generated by the transmitting coil can be approximated as static magnetic fields, which simplifies the analysis and design of the communication system. This approximation is valid when the distance between the transmitter and receiver (i.e., the complete magnetic circuit) is much smaller than the signal wavelength. In the very low frequency (VLF) range used for TTE communication, the wavelength is several tens of kilometers, and the radiation of electromagnetic waves is negligible.
\newp

Ampère's Law, in the context of magnetostatics, provides the fundamental relationship between a circulating magnetic field (B) and the electric current (J) that produces it. Specifically, the differential form states that the curl of the magnetic field is directly proportional to the current density \cite{griffiths2023introduction}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_ampere_law}
    \nabla \times B = \mu_{0} J
\end{equation}

This law is particularly useful for calculating magnetic fields when physical symmetry is present, serving a role analogous to Gauss's Law in electrostatics. However, this formulation holds strictly only for steady currents, as applying the divergence to the magnetostatic formulation reveals an inconsistency when the charge density changes over time. James Clerk Maxwell resolved this theoretical flaw by incorporating the displacement current term.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_ampere_maxwell}
    \nabla \times B = \mu_{0} J + \mu_{0} \epsilon_{0} \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}
\end{equation}

This extended form, known as the Ampère-Maxwell Law, accounts for time-varying electric fields and is essential for describing electromagnetic wave propagation. In the context of magnetic induction communication systems, this law underpins the generation and behavior of magnetic fields produced by time-varying currents in transmitting coils.
\newp
The simplest approximation for the magnetic field created by a small loop antenna (using a quasi-static field approximation) is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_magnetic_field}
    H_{qe}=\frac{m_{d}}{4\pi r^{3}}\{2~cos(\theta)\hat{r}+sin(\theta)\hat{\theta}\} ,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $m_{d}$ is the magnetic dipole moment of the transmitting coil, $R$ is the radius of the coil, $r$ is the distance from the coil center to the observation point, and $\theta$ is the angle between the coil axis and the line connecting the coil center to the observation point.

\begin{equation}
    m_d=N_{tx}I_{tx}\pi R^{2}
    \label{eq:magnetic_dipole_moment}
\end{equation}

%Add equation for Magnetic flux at a distance D from a circular coil of radius a, N turns, carrying current I:
Faraday's law of induction states that the electromotive force (EMF) induced
around a closed loop equals the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux
$\Phi$ through the surface bounded by that loop~\cite{griffiths2023introduction}.
In integral form,
\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_faraday_law}
    \varepsilon = \oint_{C} \mathbf{E}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}
                = -\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t},
    \qquad
    \Phi = \int_{S} \mathbf{B}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ is the voltage induced in the loop. For a coil of $N$ turns the
induced voltage becomes $\varepsilon = -N\,\mathrm{d}\Phi/\mathrm{d}t$. This is the
principle underlying the receiving antenna: a time-varying magnetic field produces
a voltage across the receiver coil, as quantified in Eq.~\eqref{equation_of_magnetic_induction}.
\newp

The line integral of the electric field in
Eq.~\eqref{equation_of_faraday_law} is, by definition, the induced EMF, that is,
the voltage $\varepsilon$ developed around the loop; it is therefore not carried as a
separate term into the expression below, but is precisely the induced voltage
$V_{rx}$ being sought. Writing the flux as
$\Phi = \int_{S}\mu\,\mathbf{H}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S}$ (with $\mathbf{B}=\mu\mathbf{H}$),
accounting for the $N_{rx}$ turns of the receiving coil, and taking the time
derivative of a field with harmonic dependence $e^{j\omega t}$ —which replaces
$\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t$ by the factor $j\omega$— yields the induced voltage at the
receiver.


As a result, the voltage induced at the antenna due to the magnetic field is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_magnetic_induction}
    \begin{split}
        V_{rx}(\omega) &= -j\omega N_{rx}\int_{S}\mu H\cdot dS \\
                       &= -j\omega\mu N_{rx}S_{rx}H \cos(\varphi) ,
    \end{split} 
\end{equation}

\noindent where $N_{rx}$ and $S_{rx}$ are the number of turns and the area of the receiving loop, respectively, and $\varphi$ is the angle between the magnetic field and the loop axis, which is orthogonal to its plane 
\cite{carreno2016through}.

\subsection{ Mutual Inductance and Coupling Coefficient}\label{subsec:mutual-inductance}



The mutual inductance $(M)$ between two coils (Loop 1 and Loop 2) is defined as the constant of proportionality relating the magnetic flux ($\psi$) passing through the secondary coil $(S)$ to the current $(I)$ flowing in the primary coil (P).
\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_mutual_inductance}
    M = \frac{\psi_{S}}{I_{P}} ,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $M$ is a purely geometric parameter that depends on the shape and size of the two coils. For two parallel circular coils centered on a single axis, where $r_P$ and $r_s$ are the primary and secondary radii, $N_P$ and $N_S$ are the number of turns, and $d$ is the distance separating them, M is approximated by:
\begin{equation}
    M = \frac{\mu N_{P} N_{S} \pi r_{P}^{2} r_{S}^{2}}{2 (r_{P}^{2} + d^{2})^{3/2}}
\end{equation}
%%only if rs << rP and rs??

The coupling coefficient $k$ is a dimensionless parameter, bounded by
$0 \le k \le 1$, that quantifies the strength of the magnetic coupling between the
two coils. It is defined in terms of the mutual inductance $M$ and the
self-inductances $L_1$ and $L_2$ of the coils as
\begin{equation}
    k = \frac{M}{\sqrt{L_1 L_2}}.
    \label{eq:coupling_coefficient}
\end{equation}
The power transferred across the link scales with $k^2$ (and with the product of
the coil quality factors), so the coupling coefficient is a primary determinant of
link efficiency~\cite{agbinya2022principles}. In TTE applications the coils are
comparatively small and widely separated, so only a small fraction of the
transmitted flux is intercepted by the receiver; the system is therefore loosely
coupled, with a coupling coefficient on the order of $0.1$ (about $10\,\%$) or
less~\cite{agbinya2022principles}.

\subsection{Resonance and Quality Factor}
\label{sec:resonance_q}

A magnetic-induction coil is electrically small at the very low frequencies used
for TTE communication, so on its own it behaves as an almost purely reactive
element and transfers very little power at the operating frequency. To make the
link efficient, the coil reactance is cancelled by adding a capacitor, forming an
$RLC$ resonant circuit. Resonance occurs when the inductive and capacitive
reactances are equal in magnitude, $X_L = X_C$, so that the reactive part of the
impedance vanishes and the circuit becomes purely resistive. This condition
defines the resonant (angular) frequency \cite{agbinya2022principles}.
\begin{equation}
    \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad
    f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}},
    \label{eq:resonant_frequency}
\end{equation}

The selectivity and energy-storage capability of the resonator are quantified by
its quality factor $Q$, defined as $2\pi$ times the ratio of the energy stored to
the energy dissipated per cycle.
\begin{equation}
    Q = 2\pi\,\frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy dissipated per cycle}}
      = \frac{\text{reactance}}{\text{resistance}}.
    \label{eq:q_definition}
\end{equation}
For a coil of inductance $L$, tuning capacitance $C$ and series loss resistance
$R$, this leads to the equivalent expressions:
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 R C}
      = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}.
    \label{eq:q_forms}
\end{equation}
The quality factor is also directly related to the half-power bandwidth of the
resonator through $BW = f_0/Q$, a high $Q$
produces a sharp, narrow-band response with strong field and voltage build-up,
whereas a low $Q$ widens the bandwidth at the expense of selectivity and stored
energy \cite{sadiku, jiang2015capacity}. This trade-off is central to TTE design, because
a high $Q$ improves coupling and signal-to-noise ratio but limits the usable
bandwidth and therefore the achievable symbol rate and number of orthogonal
FSK tones.

\subsubsection*{Series and parallel topologies}

The way the tuning capacitor is connected, in series or in parallel with the coil,
determines how the resonator behaves and which quantity is amplified at
resonance. In a \emph{series} $RLC$ circuit the impedance is given by:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{series}} = R + j\!\left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right),
    \label{eq:z_series}
\end{equation}
which reaches its minimum value at resonance,
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{series}}\big|_{\omega_0} = R.
    \label{eq:z_series_res}
\end{equation}
The current is therefore maximised, and the voltages across the inductor and
capacitor are amplified by a factor $Q$ with respect to the input,
$V_L = V_C = Q\,V_{in}$. The series-resonant circuit thus acts as a
\emph{voltage amplifier} and, because it presents a low impedance, allows the
power amplifier to drive the maximum possible current into the coil. This is the
preferred configuration for the \emph{transmitter}, since the radiated magnetic
moment is proportional to the coil current.

In a \emph{parallel} $RLC$ circuit the behaviour is dual. At resonance the
impedance is maximised and, for a tank whose losses are dominated by the series
resistance $R$ of the coil, it can be approximated by:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{parallel}}\big|_{\omega_0} \approx \frac{L}{RC} = Q^2 R.
    \label{eq:z_parallel_res}
\end{equation}
The current drawn from the source is minimised, but a large current circulates in
the $LC$ tank and the voltage developed across it is amplified by $Q$. The
parallel-resonant circuit therefore acts as a \emph{current amplifier} and
presents a high impedance, which makes it the preferred configuration for the
\emph{receiver}: it maximises the voltage delivered to a high-impedance front end
and improves impedance matching to the acquisition stage.
\newp
The combination of a series-tuned transmitter and a parallel-tuned receiver, known
as the series--parallel topology, maximises both the transmitted magnetic flux and
the received voltage, and is the standard choice for inductive
links \cite{agbinya2022principles}. Finally, the role of $Q$ extends to the complete link:
at resonance the power delivered to the receiver load scales with the product of
the transmitter and receiver quality factors,
\begin{equation}
    P_L = P_S\,\eta_T\,\eta_R\,Q_T\,Q_R\,k^2,
    \label{eq:power_q}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_T$ and $\eta_R$ are the transmitter and receiver efficiencies, $k$ is
the coupling coefficient, and $Q_T$, $Q_R$ are the corresponding quality
factors \cite{agbinya2012power}. This shows explicitly that, for a fixed coupling,
increasing the quality factors is one of the few available means of improving the
power transfer of a weakly coupled TTE link.

\subsection{Transfer Function of the Inductive Coupling Model}
\label{sec:transfer_function}

The end-to-end response of the link can be obtained from the equivalent circuit
of two magnetically coupled resonant loops. Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law to
the transmitter and receiver meshes, which are coupled through the mutual
inductance $M$, and solving for the voltage delivered to the load yields the
voltage transfer function \cite{agbinya2022principles, yates2004}.
\begin{equation}
    H(s) = \frac{V_{out}(s)}{V_{in}(s)}
         = \frac{s M R_L}{Z_1(s)\,Z_2(s) - (sM)^2},
    \label{eq:transfer_function}
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the mutual inductance between the two coils and $R_L$ is the load
resistance at the receiver. The terms $Z_1(s)$ and $Z_2(s)$ are the
self-impedances of the transmitter and receiver loops, each modelled as a series
$RLC$ circuit,
\begin{equation}
    Z_1(s) = R_1 + sL_1 + \frac{1}{sC_1},
    \label{eq:z1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    Z_2(s) = R_2 + sL_2 + \frac{1}{sC_2} + R_L.
    \label{eq:z2}
\end{equation}
The term $(sM)^2$ in the denominator of Eq. \eqref{eq:transfer_function}
represents the impedance reflected from the receiver back into the transmitter
through the magnetic coupling, which is what makes the response of the two loops
interdependent. It is worth noting the sign convention: because $s = j\omega$, one
has $(sM)^2 = -\omega^2 M^2$, so the same denominator may equivalently be written
in the frequency domain as $Z_1(j\omega)\,Z_2(j\omega) + \omega^2 M^2$; the two
forms are identical and must not be combined.
\newp
Evaluated on the imaginary axis, $|H(j\omega)|$ exhibits the band-pass shape
characteristic of coupled resonators: it peaks near the common resonant frequency
of the loops, where the reactive parts of $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ cancel and the
denominator is minimised, and rolls off on either side at a rate governed by the
loop quality factors. This is the behaviour
reproduced by the simulated and measured transfer functions of the constructed
system in Chapter \ref{ch:results}.

\subsection{Coil Antenna Design Considerations}
\label{sec:coil_design}

The transmitting coil is characterized by its magnetic dipole moment
\ref{eq:magnetic_dipole_moment}; extending the range of an
MI link therefore amounts to maximising $m_d$ through the number of turns
$N_{tx}$, the excitation current $I_{tx}$, or the loop area $S_{tx}$. Each of these
choices, however, carries a cost that couples coil design to the resonant
behaviour.
\newp
The coil self-inductance grows rapidly with the number of turns. For a multi-turn
circular loop of radius $R$ wound with wire of radius $r$, it can be
approximated by \cite{utfsm}:
\begin{equation}
    L = \mu\, R\, N^{2}\left[\ln\!\left(\frac{8R}{r}\right) - 2\right],
    \label{eq:coil_inductance}
\end{equation}
so $L \propto N^{2}$. The coil loss resistance, in turn, is not simply the
direct-current value: at the operating frequency the current is confined to a
surface layer of the conductor of thickness
$\delta_w = \sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma_{w})}$ by the skin effect, and the fields of
neighbouring turns add a proximity-effect contribution, so that
\begin{equation}
    R = R_{\mathrm{dc}} + R_{\mathrm{skin}} + R_{\mathrm{prox}},
    \label{eq:coil_resistance}
\end{equation}
with both frequency-dependent terms growing with the total wire length and hence
with $N$ \cite{9205830}. Because the quality factor is
$Q = \omega L / R$, and increasing the number of
turns raises $L$ as $N^{2}$ but $R$ roughly linearly, there is a design optimum
rather than a monotonic benefit. Moreover, for a target moment the power
dissipated in the winding is $P = R\,I_{tx}^{2}$, which sets a practical ceiling
on $m_d$ in a power-limited underground system \cite{carreno2016through}.

A further constraint is the self-resonant frequency of the coil. The distributed
capacitance $C_p$ between adjacent turns resonates with the self-inductance at
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{SRF}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L\,C_p}},
    \label{eq:srf}
\end{equation}
above which the coil ceases to behave as an inductor; many-turn coils have a lower
$f_{\mathrm{SRF}}$, which bounds the usable operating band. Finally, where physical
size is constrained, a high-permeability ferrite core increases the effective
moment of the transmitter, or the sensitivity of the receiver, for a compact
winding; this is the basis of the ferrite-rod receiving antennas common in TTE
systems, at the cost of higher power consumption and possible core
nonlinearity \cite{carreno2016through, agbinya2022principles}. These considerations directly inform the coil built for this work.

\section{Link Budget and System Margin}
\label{sec:link_budget}

A link budget chains the transmitter, the channel and the receiver to predict the
signal-to-noise ratio available at detection, and compares it with the minimum
required for reliable demodulation.
On the common coaxial axis ($\theta = 0$), the quasi-static field of
Eq. \eqref{equation_of_magnetic_field} reduces to $H = m_d / (2\pi r^{3})$, which in a
conductive medium is further attenuated by the factor $e^{-r/\delta}$. Substituting this field into the induced-voltage
expression of Eq. \eqref{equation_of_magnetic_induction} gives the open-circuit voltage at the
receiver,
\begin{equation}
    |V_{rx}| = \frac{\omega\mu\,N_{rx} S_{rx}\cos\varphi}{2\pi r^{3}}\;m_d\;e^{-r/\delta}.
    \label{eq:lb_voltage}
\end{equation}
When both coils are tuned to resonance, the receiver tank amplifies this voltage
by its quality factor and the transmitter current is enhanced by its own, so that
the received power scales with the product $Q_{tx}Q_{rx}k^{2}$
(Eq. \eqref{eq:power_q}). Two features of Eq. \eqref{eq:lb_voltage} dominate the
budget: the induced voltage falls as $r^{-3}$, so the received \emph{power} falls
as $r^{-6}$, a far steeper distance dependence than in far-field radio
links \cite{agbinya2013, azad2012}; and, in the ground, the exponential term adds
the frequency-dependent attenuation that forces operation at very low frequencies.

The available signal-to-noise ratio then follows by referring the received signal
power to the external noise floor of Section \ref{sec:noise_vlf},
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{avail}} = P_{rx} - P_n, \qquad
    P_n = F_a + 10\log_{10} B - 204 \ \text{[dBW]},
    \label{eq:lb_snr}
\end{equation}
where all terms are in decibels. The minimum required ratio,
$\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{req}}$, is obtained from the symbol-error-rate analysis of
Section \ref{subsec:ber-awgn}: for a target SER and a given order $M$, the required
$E_b/N_0$ maps to an SNR through Eq. \ref{eq:ebn0_snr}. The link is viable when
the margin
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M} = \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{avail}} - \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{req}}
    \label{eq:lb_margin}
\end{equation}
is positive. This budget makes explicit the levers available to the designer: a
large magnetic moment and high quality factor raise $\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{avail}}$
(Sections \ref{sec:coil_design} and \ref{sec:resonance_q}), while a narrow
bandwidth and forward error correction (Section \ref{sec:error-control-coding}) lower
$\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{req}}$.

\subsection{Skin Depth and Frequency-Dependent Attenuation in Rock}\label{subsec:skin-depth}

Influence on Attenuation and Penetration: The propagation of the magnetic field in a conductive medium is governed by the magnetic permeability ($\mu$) of that medium. The penetration depth is quantified by the skin depth ($\delta$), which is inversely related to permeability:
\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_skin_depth}
    \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega \mu \sigma}} , 
\end{equation}
 \noindent where $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the signal, and $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity of the medium. In geological materials like rock, properties can vary widely depending on composition. When the medium contains ferromagnetic materials with high permeability, the skin depth decreases. This means the signal experiences greater attenuation and a shorter effective communication range, as energy transfer relies heavily on magnetic-field diffusion.

\subsection{Geometric Configuration: Coil Orientation, Separation, Misalignment}\label{subsec:geometry}

The geometric configuration of the transmitter and receiver coils significantly impacts the performance of a TTE magnetic induction communication system. The relative orientation, separation distance, and potential misalignment between the coils can affect the mutual inductance and coupling coefficient, thereby influencing the strength of the received signal. Optimal alignment typically occurs when the coils' axes are parallel, and their centers are aligned, maximizing magnetic flux linkage. However, in practical scenarios, especially in underground environments, perfect alignment may not be achievable, leading to reduced coupling and increased signal attenuation.
\newp
As is clear from \ref{equation_of_magnetic_induction} equation, the voltage induced at the receiver coil is proportional to the cosine of the angle ($\varphi$) between the magnetic field and the loop axis. The angle between the magnetic field and the loop axis could change depending on two types of misalignment: angular misalignment, where the coils are rotated relative to each other, and lateral misalignment, where the centers of the coils are not perfectly aligned but in parallel planes.
\newp
In the ideal case, the flux density is maximum, and both coils are perfectly aligned. The flux density for a single-turn circular coil of radius $a$ carrying current $I$ at a distance $D$ along the axis is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_flux_density_ideal}
    H_{ideal} = \frac{ I_{tx} a^{2}}{2 (a^{2} + D^{2})^{3/2}}
\end{equation}

In the case of angular misalignment, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:angular-misalignment}, the flux density is reduced and the expression is given by \cite{fotopoulou2007optimum}:

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_flux_density_angular_misalignment}
    H = \frac{I_{tx}\, a^2 \cos\gamma}{2\left(a^2 + D^2\right)^{3/2}}
      = H_{ideal}\cos\gamma .
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/eqs/angular.png}
    \caption{Diagram illustrating angular misalignment between transmitter and receiver coils. Extracted from \cite{fotopoulou2007optimum}.}
    \label{fig:angular-misalignment}
\end{figure}

In case of lateral misalignment, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:lateral-misalignment}, the flux density is also reduced and the expression is given by \cite{fotopoulou2007optimum}:

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation_of_flux_density_lateral_misalignment}
    H = \frac{I_{tx}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}
        \frac{a^2 - a\Delta\cos\varphi'}
             {\left(\sqrt{\,a^2 + D^2 + \Delta^2 - 2a\Delta\cos\varphi'\,}\right)^{3}}
        \, d\varphi' .
\end{equation}

\section{Overview of Through-The-Earth (TTE) Magnetic Induction Communication}

This chapter presents the theoretical foundations and conceptual framework for achieving effective through-rock communication. It covers the physical principles of magnetic induction, the characteristics of the geological medium, channel modeling, transducer (coil) design, and the fundamentals of digital communication applied to TTE systems. Furthermore, relevant digital signal processing (DSP) techniques are discussed, and the main technical challenges associated with TTE communication using magnetic induction are identified.

\subsection{System Architecture}\label{subsec:system-architecture}
% (From: TTE Communication System Architecture)
% TODO: High-level block diagram: Transmitter coil, power amplifier, matching network, channel (rock), receiver coil, LNA, filtering, demodulation, decoding.

The typical architecture of a TTE magnetic induction communication system consists of a transmitter and receiver, each equipped with resonant coils designed to operate at low frequencies. The transmitter generates a time-varying current that produces a magnetic field, which propagates through the geological medium. The receiver coil captures the magnetic flux, inducing a voltage that is processed to recover the transmitted information. Key components include power amplifiers, impedance-matching networks, filters, demodulators, and error-correction decoders.

\subsection{Key Technical Challenges (Preview)}\label{subsec:key-challenges-preview}
% (From: Main technical challenges, brief intro; detailed in Section \ref{sec:challenges-limitations})
% TODO: Severe attenuation, limited bandwidth, alignment sensitivity, coil size constraints, interference, power budget.

Communication through the rock is particularly challenging due to several technical factors. Here are some of the main challenges:

\begin{itemize}
     \item \textbf{Severe Attenuation and Limited Bandwidth:} The dense media significantly attenuate electromagnetic signals, especially at higher frequencies; it is necessary to use very low frequencies (VLF), typically leading to limited bandwidth and data rates. \cite{carreno2016through}
 
    \item \textbf{Channel Instability and Fading:} The channel power gain is often unstable, leading to "remarkably fast fading" caused by the uncertainty of the medium over large spaces and the vibration of mobile antennas. This unpredictability complicates existing wireless communication system solutions.

    \item \textbf{Alignment Sensitivity:} The performance of magnetic induction links is highly dependent on the relative orientation and alignment of the transmitter and receiver coils.
 
    \item \textbf{Coil Size Constraints and Power Consumption:} To achieve sufficient magnetic flux, coils often need to be large, which can be impractical for portable or deployable systems. Greater power means more magnetic flux. Limited power availability in underground environments necessitates energy-efficient system designs.
 
    \item \textbf{Interference and Noise:} External electromagnetic interference and geological noise sources can degrade signal quality.
    \item \textbf{Power Budget:} Limited power availability in underground environments necessitates energy-efficient system designs.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{State of the Art Overview}
\label{sec:state_of_art}

Research on TTE magnetic-induction communication has been driven largely by
post-accident mine-safety requirements, and spans three strands: early research
prototypes, commercially available products, and a growing body of recent
academic work aimed at improving range, capacity and
deployability \cite{ngwenyama2024, ma2025survey}.
\newp
Much of the foundational work originated from
programmes coordinated by NIOSH, which produced prototypes from at least five
companies (Alertek, E-Spectrum Technologies, Lockheed Martin, Stolar and Ultra
Electronics). Four were based on magnetic-field detection with loop antennas and
one on electric-field detection. They demonstrated unidirectional and, in some
cases, bidirectional voice and text communication at depths up to roughly 300~m
for voice and 600~m for text, operating between about 3150 and 4820~Hz, with
analog single-sideband or digital PSK/FSK modulation, and in some cases
noise-cancellation techniques \cite{carreno2016through}. An early two-way system,
TeleMag, used a 4~kHz carrier with a 20~m loop antenna \cite{bae2013}.

\paragraph{Commercial systems.} A few systems have reached the market, though all
remain limited in range, portability or cost:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Flex Alert} (MineRadio Systems, Canada): a unidirectional
        surface-to-underground system for emergency evacuation that flashes
        miners' helmet lamps via a large surface loop antenna \cite{carreno2016through}.
  \item \emph{Personal Emergency Device (PED)} (MineSite Technologies, Australia):
        a unidirectional downlink system for text/SMS messaging, which can be
        paired with a leaky feeder for the uplink and is also used for remote
        blasting and equipment control \cite{carreno2016through}.
  \item \emph{MagneLink MCS} (Lockheed Martin) \cite{teslasociety}: a self-contained bidirectional
        system supporting digital voice, text and localisation, validated to
        500~m depth with a 130~m surface antenna, and the first TTE two-way
        voice/text system to obtain U.S. MSHA safety approval; it is, however,
        heavy and bulky \cite{carreno2016through, ngwenyama2024}.
  \item \emph{Canary / CanaryCommPac} (Vital Alert, Canada) \cite{vitalalert}: a bidirectional
        digital voice and data system whose receiver is implemented in
        software-defined radio, reconfigurable between 300~Hz and 9~kHz with
        adaptive modulation from 9~bps to 1~kbps. It is compact and portable
        (8-hour battery life) but has a short native range (around 50~m,
        extendable to 300~m with a large external antenna), is expensive (on the
        order of USD~30{,}000), and has limited availability outside the
        U.S. \cite{carreno2016through}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Recent academic advances.} Recent research targets the fundamental
range, antenna-size and power limitations of TTE links. To strengthen the
transmitted field without bulky loops, alternative field generators have been
proposed, including pulsed ferrite-core generators \cite{bae2013} and mechanical
(motion-based) magnetic field generators operating in the ULF
range \cite{rezaei2020}. To extend range and coverage, cooperative
magnetic-induction and relaying schemes, as well as metamaterial-enhanced coils,
have been investigated \cite{ma2025survey}. More recent work addresses emergency
multi-user scenarios through multi-frequency resonant designs and
heterogeneous-transceiver architectures that improve flexibility and distance for
surface-to-multiple-node rescue communication \cite{wang2025}. In parallel,
comprehensive surveys have begun to formalise MI channel modelling, the recently
identified phenomenon of MI fast fading, and the integration of TTE links into
broader underground sensor networks \cite{ma2025survey}.
\newp
Within this landscape, the present work is a complete, low-cost, software-defined
TTE system based on non-coherent FSK and Reed--Solomon coding, aimed at the
portability and affordability gaps left by existing commercial solutions.

\subsection{Electrical Properties of Rock: Conductivity, Permittivity, Permeability}
\label{subsec:rock-properties}

The propagation of magnetic fields in underground environments is governed by
three electrical properties of the soil and rock: the electrical conductivity
$\sigma$, the permittivity $\varepsilon$, and the magnetic permeability $\mu$. Each
plays a distinct role at the very low frequencies used for TTE communication.

The magnetic permeability is the property that makes magnetic induction attractive
underground. Most geological materials---soil, rock and water---are essentially
non-magnetic, with a permeability very close to that of free space,
$\mu_0 \approx 4\pi\times 10^{-7}$~H/m. As a result the ground is, to first order,
``transparent'' to the magnetic field, which passes through it with little
additional attenuation; this is a fundamental advantage of MI over
electric-field-based communication, whose fields are strongly attenuated by the
medium~\cite{abrudan2016}.

The dominant loss mechanism is instead the electrical conductivity. Conductivity
gives rise to eddy currents that dissipate energy and attenuate the field, and it
governs the skin depth of Eq.~\eqref{equation_of_skin_depth}. Its value varies by
many orders of magnitude between materials and depends strongly on water content,
since conduction occurs mainly through the interconnected pore fluid rather than
through the mineral grains. Nevertheless, at the very low frequencies of interest
the skin effect remains small for most common rocks and soils, which is precisely
what allows the field to penetrate over useful distances~\cite{abrudan2016}.

The permittivity is best treated as a complex quantity, and the relative
permittivity is commonly written as
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_r = \varepsilon_r' - j\,\frac{\sigma}{\omega \varepsilon_0},
    \label{eq:complex_permittivity}
\end{equation}
where the real part $\varepsilon_r'$ describes polarisation and the imaginary part
captures the conduction losses associated with $\sigma$. The real part typically
ranges from a few units to a few tens and, like the conductivity, increases with
water content (water alone has $\varepsilon_r \approx 80$). However, at TTE
frequencies the contribution of the permittivity to the skin depth is negligible
compared with that of the conductivity, so it plays only a minor
role~\cite{abrudan2016}.

In practice the medium is rarely homogeneous or isotropic. The ground is usually
stratified into layers (for example sandstone, coal or siltstone) with distinct
electrical and magnetic properties, and this layering introduces anisotropy in the
propagation~\cite{9205830}. Of particular importance are iron-rich, ferromagnetic
minerals such as magnetite, whose relative permeability is well above unity: they
support high flux densities for a given magnetising field and therefore tend to
concentrate and guide the magnetic flux along preferred paths. This locally
distorts the field, which reduces the effective communication range and can also
bias magneto-inductive localisation~\cite{abrudan2016, ma2025survey}.

\subsection{Propagation Models in Conductive Geological Media}\label{subsec:propagation-models}
% (From: Magnetic propagation models in conductive media)
% TODO: Analytical models, homogeneous vs layered media, empirical fits.


The penetration of the magnetic field H in a conductive medium like rock with a high ferrite concentration follows the diffusion equation $\nabla^{2}H=\mu\sigma\partial H/\partial t$, instead of the wave equation $\nabla^{2}H=\mu\epsilon(\partial^{2}H)/(\partial t^{2})$, with an exponentially decaying field as a function of distance, angular frequency $\omega$ and electric conductivity of the propagation medium. In a good conductor (with $\sigma(\epsilon\omega)>>1)$ the signal attenuation is described by the skin depth \ref{equation_of_skin_depth}.  \cite{carreno2016through}. That inverse square root dependency on the frequency justifies the use of low frequencies, usually below 30 kHz for TTE communication.

\newp
Most minerals have negligible magnetic permeability, so the skin depth depends primarily on the conductivity of the medium. This also depends on the material's porosity and moisture content. A complete model of the skin depth is given by \cite{abrudan2016impactrocksmineralsunderground}:

\begin{equation}
    \delta = \left[ \omega \sqrt{ \frac{\mu \varepsilon^{\prime}}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + \left( \frac{\sigma}{\omega \varepsilon^{\prime}} \right)^{2}} - 1 \right)} \right]^{ -1 }
\end{equation}

The following  figure shows the skin depth for different materials at different frequencies.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{img/diagrams/skin.png}
    \caption{Skin depth for different materials at different frequencies. Extracted from \cite{abrudan2016impactrocksmineralsunderground}.}
    \label{fig:skin_depth}
\end{figure}
\newp

Even when using a low frequency the decay of magnetic field is less, there is a trade-off between the frequency and the bandwidth of the system. Bandwidth depends on the central frequency and the quality factor, which is inversely proportional to the frequency and bandwidth.

\begin{equation}
    BW = \frac{f_0}{Q}
\end{equation}

Therefore, the bandwidth increases with the central frequency, but it reduces with quality factor, that improves efficiency and coupling \cite{jiang2015capacity}. It is necessary to find an optimal frequency that balances penetration depth and achievable data rates.
\newp

The medium can also be inhomogeneous and anisotropic, which can cause significant variability in the channel characteristics. In such cases, more complex models that account for stratification and directional dependencies of the material properties are necessary to accurately predict signal propagation and design effective communication systems. For example if there is rock and air in the path, that have very different electrical properties, the channel exhibits non-reciprocity due to different transmission coefficients \cite{9205830}.

\section{Noise in the VLF/ULF Communication Channel}
\label{sec:noise_vlf}

Unlike conventional radio systems, where thermal noise from the receiver front
end is the dominant impairment, TTE magnetic-induction links are limited by the
external electromagnetic environment. Because the signal is strongly attenuated
as it diffuses through the conductive ground while external noise reaches the
receiver largely unattenuated, it is the captured noise, rather than the
receiver, that sets the achievable signal-to-noise ratio \cite{ma2025survey}. Two
external sources are relevant in this band: atmospheric noise and man-made
noise \cite{carreno2016through}.

\emph{Atmospheric noise} originates from lightning discharges that radiate
impulsive energy propagating with low loss in the Earth--ionosphere waveguide. It
is usually described as a Gaussian background plus an impulsive component, and is
dominant roughly between 10 and 30~kHz \cite{carreno2016through}. Several statistical
models exist for its amplitude-probability distribution, but a detailed treatment
is outside the scope of this work: at the operating frequency and in the confined
underground sites considered here, atmospheric noise was not a limiting factor in
the measurements, which were instead dominated by man-made interference.

\subsection{Man-made Noise}
\label{sec:manmade_noise}

The dominant interference observed in this work comes from the harmonics of the
50/60~Hz power distribution system, radiated by electrical machinery and
transmission lines. These harmonics are stronger at low frequencies and, because
the underlying loads modulate them, they are not pure spectral lines but narrow
bands centred on each multiple of the fundamental; they remain detectable even at
distances on the order of a kilometre from the nearest power
line \cite{carreno2016through}. This behaviour is consistent with the spectra measured
during this work, in which several strong harmonic components fall within the
transmission band (Chapter \ref{ch:discussion}).

The narrowband, structured nature of power-line harmonics also makes them
tractable: unlike broadband noise, they can be attenuated by notch filtering,
spectral subtraction, or adaptive cancellation techniques developed specifically
for low-frequency communication
channels \cite{cohen2010, wang2018adaptive}. The spectral-subtraction approach adopted in
this thesis exploits precisely this property, estimating the harmonic noise
spectrum and removing it prior to demodulation, as detailed in
Chapter \ref{ch:discussion}.

\subsection{Noise Quantification}
\label{sec:noise_quantification}

The reference for radio-noise levels is Recommendation ITU-R~P.372, which
characterises atmospheric, man-made and galactic noise from 0.1~Hz upward through
an external noise factor $F_a$, defined relative to thermal noise at the reference
temperature $T_0 = 290$~K \cite{itur_p372}.
\begin{equation}
    F_a = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{T_a}{T_0}\right) \ \text{[dB]},
    \label{eq:noise_factor}
\end{equation}
where $T_a$ is the equivalent antenna noise temperature. The available external
noise power in a bandwidth $B$ is then
\begin{equation}
    P_n = F_a + 10\log_{10} B - 204 \ \text{[dBW]}.
    \label{eq:noise_power}
\end{equation}
In the VLF/ULF range $F_a$ reaches several tens of decibels, confirming that the
external environment, not the receiver, governs the link budget. Because this
noise is non-Gaussian and contains strong deterministic-like harmonic components,
the AWGN-optimal detectors and symbol-error-rate bounds are no longer optimal, which motivates the harmonic mitigation and forward error correction (Section \ref{subsec:block-codes}) used in this system.

\subsection{Channel Capacity}
\label{sec:capacity}

The maximum rate at which information can be transmitted error-free over a channel
of bandwidth $B$ corrupted by additive noise is bounded by the Shannon--Hartley
theorem.
\begin{equation}
    C = B \log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right) \quad \text{[bits/s]},
    \label{eq:capacity}
\end{equation}
where $S/N$ is the signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver. Equation \eqref{eq:capacity}
makes explicit why TTE magnetic-induction links are inherently low-rate: capacity
grows only \emph{logarithmically} with $S/N$ but \emph{linearly} with bandwidth,
and in this channel both factors are unfavourable. The severe attenuation through
the ground and the high external noise keep $S/N$
low, while the use of high-$Q$ resonant coils to recover signal level
(Section \ref{sec:resonance_q}) restricts the available bandwidth to
$B = f_0/Q$. These two effects are in direct tension: raising $Q$ improves $S/N$
but narrows $B$, so there exists an operating point that maximises capacity rather
than a monotonic benefit from increasing either quantity \cite{jing2008, agbinya2012power}.
This trade-off underlies the choice of operating frequency, bandwidth and
modulation order adopted in this work.

\section{Digital Signal Processing for the Receiver}
\label{sec:dsp}

In a software-defined receiver, the magnetic field captured by the coil is
digitised at the acquisition stage, and every subsequent operation is performed
numerically. Before the transmitted symbols can be detected, the acquired signal
must be conditioned: confined to the band of interest, cleared of out-of-band
interference, and brought to a sample rate suited to real-time processing. These
operations rest on a small set of digital signal processing fundamentals, the
sampling theorem, multirate processing, and digital filtering, which are
summarised here as the basis for the receiver chain implemented in
Chapter \ref{ch:results}. The two operations most relevant to this work,
decimation and band-pass filtering, are treated in turn; together with the
correlation detector of Section \ref{sec:noncoherent-fsk}, they constitute the
complete reception process.

\subsection{Sampling and Decimation}
\label{sec:decimation}

The received signal is digitised by the acquisition stage at a sampling rate
$f_s$ that must satisfy the Nyquist--Shannon condition $f_s \ge 2 f_{\max}$, where
$f_{\max}$ is the highest frequency present, to avoid aliasing \cite{lyons2011}.
In this system the information occupies a narrow band around the operating
frequency $f_0$, far below the Nyquist limit of the acquisition rate, so
processing every sample at the full rate is computationally wasteful, an important
consideration for the real-time correlation receiver.
\newp
Decimation reduces the sample rate by an integer factor $D$ while preserving the
band of interest. It consists of two operations: a digital anti-aliasing low-pass
filter, with cut-off below the new Nyquist frequency $f_s/2D$, followed by
downsampling that retains every $D$-th sample,
\begin{equation}
    y[m] = x_{\mathrm{LP}}[mD], \qquad f_s' = \frac{f_s}{D}.
    \label{eq:decimation}
\end{equation}
The anti-aliasing filter is essential: downsampling without it would fold
out-of-band components back into the signal band \cite{lyons2011}. By lowering the
working rate to $f_s'$, decimation proportionally reduces the number of operations
in the correlation stage, which is the dominant cost of the detection algorithm.


\subsection{Digital Bandpass Filtering}
\label{sec:filtering}

Before detection, a digital band-pass filter isolates the modulation band around
$f_0$ and rejects out-of-band interference, in particular the low-frequency
power-line harmonics discussed in Section \ref{sec:noise_vlf}. Digital filters are
realised in two families. A finite impulse response (FIR) filter computes its
output as a weighted sum of present and past inputs,
\begin{equation}
    y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k\, x[n-k],
    \label{eq:fir}
\end{equation}
whereas an infinite impulse response (IIR) filter is recursive, also feeding back
past outputs,
\begin{equation}
    y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k\, x[n-k] - \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k\, y[n-k].
    \label{eq:iir}
\end{equation}
The two families trade off differently \cite{lyons2011}. An IIR filter achieves a
given selectivity with a much lower order, and hence less computation, but has a
nonlinear phase response and must be checked for stability. An FIR filter is
unconditionally stable and can be designed with exactly linear phase, that is, a
constant group delay that does not distort the waveform shape. The latter property
is valuable in a correlation-based receiver, where preserving the temporal
structure of the symbols protects the matched-filter operation; for this reason a linear-phase FIR band-pass filter is adopted in this work, accepting its higher order as the cost of phase
fidelity.

\section{Non-Coherent FSK Modulation for MI Communication}\label{sec:noncoherent-fsk}

Non-Coherent Frequency Shift Keying (NCFSK) is a digital bandpass modulation technique that belongs to the class of noncoherent detection methods. Unlike coherent detection, noncoherent detection simplifies receiver implementation because it does not require the receiver to track the carrier phase.
\newp
In the case of FSK, detection involves processing signals that shift between different frequencies. In a model of M-FSK, every symbol has a respective frequency $f_m$. For noncoherent orthogonal FSK specifically, there is a required tone spacing necessary for proper operation. This separation is twice the required for coherent FSK.
\newp
A detector for non-coherent M-FSK signals can be implemented using correlators. In Figure \ref{FSK_detec}, a scheme of a non-coherent binary FSK detector based on correlators is shown, this is essentially the \textit{Correlation by parts} algorithm explained in the following chapter \cite{sklar2021digital}.

\subsection{Signal Space and Frequency Separation. Orthogonality}\label{subsec:frequency-separation}
% TODO: Δf selection vs symbol duration.
The orthogonality condition means that the integral of the product of two different basis functions over the symbol period is zero. For M-FSK, this condition can be expressed mathematically as:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{0}^{T} s_i(t) s_j(t) dt = 0, \quad i \neq j
\end{equation}
where $s_i(t)$ and $s_j(t)$ are the signals corresponding to different frequencies, and T is the symbol duration. To ensure orthogonality, the frequency separation ($\Delta f$) between adjacent frequencies must satisfy the following condition:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta f = \frac{k}{T} ,
\end{equation}
where k is a positive integer (k = 1, 2, 3, ...). The most common choice is k = 1, which gives the minimum frequency separation required for orthogonality. This means that the frequencies used in M-FSK modulation should be spaced at intervals of 1/T to ensure that they are orthogonal over the symbol duration T.
\newp
Satisfying the orthogonality condition is crucial for minimizing interference between different frequency components in M-FSK modulation. When the frequencies are orthogonal, the receiver can effectively distinguish between them, leading to improved performance in terms of error rates and overall communication reliability.

\subsection{Matched Filtering and the Correlation Receiver}
\label{sec:matched_filter}

The detector structures introduced above (Figs. \ref{FSK_detec}
and \ref{FSK_detec_filterbank}) are instances of a single optimal principle:
matched filtering. Consider a received signal corrupted by additive white
Gaussian noise (AWGN),
\begin{equation}
    r(t) = s_i(t) + n(t), \qquad 0 \le t \le T,
    \label{eq:received_signal}
\end{equation}
where $n(t)$ has two-sided power spectral density $N_0/2$. The matched filter is
the linear filter that maximises the instantaneous signal-to-noise ratio at the
sampling instant $t = T$. For a known symbol waveform $s_i(t)$, its impulse
response is a time-reversed, delayed replica of the signal,
\begin{equation}
    h_i(t) = s_i(T - t),
    \label{eq:matched_filter_h}
\end{equation}
and the resulting peak signal-to-noise ratio depends only on the symbol energy
$E_i$, not on the waveform shape.
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{S}{N}\right)_{\max} = \frac{2E_i}{N_0}.
    \label{eq:mf_snr}
\end{equation}

Sampling the matched-filter output at $t = T$ is equivalent to correlating the
received signal with the symbol waveform over the symbol period,
\begin{equation}
    y_i(T) = \int_0^{T} r(t)\,s_i(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,
    \label{eq:correlation_mf}
\end{equation}
which is exactly the correlation operation used for detection and synchronisation
in this work (Eqs. \ref{eq:Correlation}--\ref{eq:Correlation_dig}). Matched
filtering and correlation are therefore two implementations of the same optimal
receiver \cite{sklar2021digital}.
\newp
For non-coherent M-FSK the carrier phase is unknown at the receiver, so a single
matched filter cannot be used directly. The optimal non-coherent receiver instead
employs, for each candidate tone $f_i$, a pair of quadrature correlators (in-phase
and quadrature, $I$ and $Q$) whose outputs are combined into an envelope
\begin{equation}
    z_i = \sqrt{\,y_{i,I}^{2} + y_{i,Q}^{2}\,}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, M,
    \label{eq:envelope}
\end{equation}
and the decision selects the tone with the largest envelope,
$\hat{s} = \arg\max_i z_i$. This is the energy/envelope detector of
Fig. \ref{FSK_detec_filterbank}, and it is the structure realised in this
system by the in-phase/quadrature correlation-by-parts algorithm described in
Chapter \ref{ch:results}. Because it discards phase information, the
non-coherent receiver incurs a small penalty with respect to its coherent
counterpart, in exchange for a substantially simpler implementation that does not
require carrier-phase tracking \cite{sklar2021digital}.

It must be emphasised that the optimality of the matched filter, and the
error-rate expressions that follow from it, are established \emph{under the AWGN
assumption}. Their validity in the real TTE channel is examined in
Section \ref{subsec:ber-awgn}.

\subsection{System-Level Synchronization and Framing}\label{sec:synchronization}

We can also try to synchronize the system. This is typically achieved through the search for a known signal called preamble that is before the message.
\newp
For time synchronization in a non-coherent M-FSK model, we can use correlation techniques between the received signal and the known reference signal. The correlation outputs are then analyzed to determine the presence of an incoming message.
\newp
The correlation operation for continuous-time signals is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Correlation}
    z_i(T) = \int_{0}^{T} r(t)s_i(t) \, dt, \quad i=1, \dots, M
\end{equation}

The version for digital signals is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Correlation_dig}
    z_i[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} r[k]s_i[k+n], \quad i=1, \dots, M
\end{equation}

\section{Error Control Coding: Reed-Solomon}\label{sec:error-control-coding}


\subsection{Role of FEC in MI Links}

TTE communication systems, often necessary for underground mines, face channels characterized by large attenuation and operate using very low frequencies (VLF). This difficult scenario imposes severe restrictions on the communication system design, including dealing with high levels of atmospheric and electrical equipment noise. This environment leads to extremely limited bandwidth and capacity in the TTE channels. Due to the presence of non-Gaussian noise and the low capacity, the use of higher-order digital modulations is often challenging. \cite{ma2025survey}.

In this context, Forward Error Correction (FEC) techniques, such as Reed-Solomon coding, become essential. FEC allows the system to detect and correct errors that occur during transmission without the need for retransmission, which is particularly beneficial in TTE scenarios where latency and reliability are critical. By adding redundancy to the transmitted data, FEC improves the robustness of the communication link against noise and interference, thereby enhancing the overall performance and reliability of TTE MI communication systems.

\subsection{ Reed--Solomon coding}\label{subsec:block-codes}

Reed-Solomon (R-S) codes constitute a powerful class of nonbinary cyclic codes. These codes utilize symbols composed of m-bit sequences, where m is any positive integer greater than two. A conventional R-S code is designated as R-S $(n,k)$, where k represents the number of data symbols being encoded, and n is the total number of code symbols in the resulting block. The relationship defining a conventional R-S code is $(n, k) = (2^{m}-1, 2^{m}-1-2t)$, where $2t = n-k$ is the number of parity symbols, and t is the code's symbol-error correcting capability \cite{sklar2001reed}.
